01 · Purpose

Why do researchers sample?

A researcher often wants to understand a large group, but studying every member may be costly, time-consuming or impractical. Sampling means selecting a subset of that population to learn about the whole population.

Population

The complete group relevant to the research question.

Example: All 4,800 M.Com students enrolled across selected colleges.

Sample

The selected units from which data are collected.

Example: 240 students selected from that population.
Simulation challenge: You are a postgraduate researcher studying digital payment adoption among M.Com students. You must choose a sampling method, estimate a sample size and defend your decisions.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish population, sampling unit, sampling frame and sample.
  • Differentiate probability and non-probability sampling methods.
  • Estimate sample size using a stated formula and assumptions.
  • Select and justify a sampling method for a research situation.
  • Recognise sampling error, bias and limitations in generalisation.

02 · Research

Understand the sampling vocabulary

Before choosing a technique, define exactly who or what can be studied.

Population

All units about which the researcher wants to draw conclusions.

Who is in scope?

Sampling unit

The basic unit selected at a particular stage, such as an individual student, household or firm.

What gets selected?

Sampling frame

A list or operational representation of the population from which selection is made.

From which list?

Sample

The subset of units actually selected for the study.

Whom will you study?

Two broad families of sampling

Probability sampling

Selection uses a random mechanism. Each eligible unit has a known, non-zero probability of selection.

Examples: simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling.

Non-probability sampling

Selection does not use random selection with known inclusion probabilities. Selection may depend on access, judgement or referrals.

Examples: convenience, purposive, quota and snowball sampling.

Important distinction: A large sample does not automatically remove selection bias. The sampling process and coverage matter, not only the number of respondents.

08 · Reflection

From a sample to a defensible conclusion

Sampling is not simply about deciding how many respondents to approach. It is about defining the target population, establishing a workable frame, choosing a defensible selection process and acknowledging limitations.

Key takeaways

  • Match the method to the research question and population.
  • Use random selection when probability-based inference is required and feasible.
  • Sample size does not compensate for a biased frame or selection process.
  • Report the method, frame, selection steps, response rate and limitations.
